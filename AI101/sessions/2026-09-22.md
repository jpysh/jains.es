## 5. FIVE BEATS

1. **Open the table.** Wrong model: the vocabulary is a secret inside the
   model. Point at: the first fifty entries of `_mergeable_ranks`, printed.
   Takeaway: it is a list of byte sequences and you can read it.
2. **A merge is a count, not a rule.** Wrong model: someone decided that "ing"
   should be one token. Point at: the rank numbers, lowest first. Takeaway:
   the pair that appeared most often got merged first, and that is the whole
   algorithm.
3. **The merge that did not happen.** Wrong model: Hindi is missing from the
   vocabulary. Point at: the Hindi word's tokens beside the English word's.
   Takeaway: the bytes are all there — what is missing is the merges that
   would have joined them.
4. **The part I am least sure about.** What counts as a fragment of a
   character in UTF-8, and therefore what I am actually counting. Devanagari
   characters take three bytes. If a token is two of those three bytes, I do
   not yet know whether to call that one failed merge or two.
5. **Someone is fixing this.** Wrong model: nothing can be done until the next
   big model. Point at: a 2025 preprint that changes which pair gets merged
   next so the worst-served language gains first. Takeaway: the merge order is
   a choice, and choosing differently is an active research direction.

Yesterday I told you that a sentence in Hindi costs about eight times what the
same sentence costs in English. Today I want to open the thing that does it,
because it is not hidden.

This is the vocabulary. It is a list. Every line is a sequence of bytes and a
number. The number is the rank: rank zero was added first, rank one second,
and so on to about a hundred thousand.

I am going to print the first fifty lines. Read them with me. They are
fragments of English words, and a few pieces of code, and that is it. That
list is a photograph of the text this tokeniser was built from.

clip

Now, how did the list get made?

Nobody sat down and decided that "ing" should be one token. The algorithm is
simpler than that, and it is almost embarrassing.

Look at all the text. Find the two adjacent pieces that occur together most
often. Glue them into one piece. Add it to the list. Then do it again. And
again, a hundred thousand times.

That is byte-pair encoding. That is the whole thing. A merge is a count, not
a rule.

So the list is not a judgement about which languages matter. It is a
frequency table. Which is, if anything, worse, because nobody is responsible
for it.

clip

Here is the part I actually wanted to show you.

Take an English word. Watch it come apart into two or three pieces, and look
at the ranks: low numbers. These merges happened early, because these pairs
were everywhere in the text.

Now take the Hindi word. More pieces. Smaller pieces. And some of them are
not even whole characters.

That last part matters. Every character your keyboard makes is already in
there, because the vocabulary starts with all two hundred and fifty-six
possible bytes. Nothing is missing.

What is missing is the merges. The bytes never got glued together, because
the pairs did not show up often enough in the text somebody chose.

Your language is not absent. Your language is unmerged.

clip

The part I am least sure about, and this is a real one.

A Devanagari character is three bytes in UTF-8. When I look at a token that
holds two of those three bytes, I do not know what to call it. Is that one
merge that failed, or two? My count changes depending on the answer, and my
count is the thing I am putting on screen.

I am going to show you my number anyway, and tell you it is provisional. If
you know the convention here, the reply link is the point of this channel.

Last thing, and it is the hopeful one.

There is a preprint from August last year that changes the rule. Instead of
always merging the most common pair overall, it merges the pair that helps
whichever language is currently worst served. The authors report cutting
tokenisation inequality by up to eighty-nine percent against ordinary
byte-pair encoding.

So the merge order is a choice. Someone is choosing differently.
