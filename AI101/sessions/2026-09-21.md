## 5. FIVE BEATS

1. **The bill, not the theory.** The wrong mental model: a model reads
   letters, so every language costs the same. Point at: two API calls, the
   same sentence, two different token counts. Takeaway: you are billed per
   token, and the token is not a letter.
2. **What a token actually is.** Wrong model: a token is a word. Point at:
   the `cl100k_base` split of the Hindi sentence, printed piece by piece,
   where single characters come out as separate tokens. Takeaway: a token is
   whatever the tokeniser's training data made common.
3. **The number for your language.** Wrong model: it is a small difference.
   Point at: the table, English row against the Hindi and Tamil rows.
   Takeaway: the multiplier is large enough to change what you can afford to
   build.
4. **The part I am least sure about.** Two things. First, whether the right
   comparison is tokens-per-sentence or tokens-per-character — my table can
   be made to say different multipliers depending on which I pick, and I do
   not yet know which one a practitioner would defend. Second, the
   curriculum said to expect roughly 4x; the paper I found reports an
   average of 8.0x across fourteen languages. I do not yet know whether the
   gap is my sentence choice, my metric, or the curriculum being out of
   date.
5. **It is a design choice, not a property of your script.** Wrong model:
   Devanagari is just expensive. Point at: the `o200k_base` column, where
   the same sentences cost less. Takeaway: someone chose the training corpus,
   and the cost followed the choice.

clip

I want to show you something about what an AI model costs you, and it starts
with a bill.

Here is one sentence. "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity
and rights." That is Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
It has official translations, so I am not comparing my English to somebody's
machine Hindi.

I sent the English version to a counter. I sent the Hindi version to the same
counter. The counts were not the same. They were not close.

Now, when you call one of these models through an API, you do not pay per
sentence. You pay per token. So if the Hindi sentence is more tokens, the
Hindi sentence costs more money. Same meaning. Same idea. More money.

clip

So what is a token?

Most people assume it is a word. It is not. Let me show you what the
tokeniser actually did to that Hindi line.

It cut it into pieces. Some pieces are several characters. Some pieces are
one character. And a few pieces are not even a whole character — they are
single bytes, because the character did not survive the split.

A tokeniser is a list of common pieces. It was built by looking at a lot of
text and keeping whatever showed up often. If your language was not much of
that text, your language is not much of that list. So your text gets cut into
smaller and smaller pieces.

Here is the analogy, and here is where it breaks. It is like a shop that
sells rice in twenty-kilo sacks and five-kilo sacks and one-kilo bags. If you
want twenty kilos, you buy one sack. If the shop has never stocked your size,
you buy twenty one-kilo bags, and you pay more for the same rice. Where it
breaks: the shop could stock your size tomorrow at no cost to itself. A
tokeniser's vocabulary is fixed when the model is trained, so it cannot.

clip

Now the number.

I counted tokens for the same sentence in English, Hindi, Bengali and Tamil.
I am putting my table on screen. I am not going to read you a figure I got
from somewhere else. This is what came out of my own file, which is in the
repository, and you can run it.

The English row is the baseline. Every other row is a multiple of it.

clip

And now the part I am least sure about, which is the part I actually want
your help with.

There are two ways to state this multiplier. You can compare tokens per
sentence, or you can compare tokens per character. They do not give the same
number. I picked one. I am not certain it is the one somebody who does this
for a living would pick, and it changes the headline figure.

Second thing. My own curriculum told me to expect about four times. There is
a paper from July this year that measures fourteen Indian languages and
reports an average of eight times under the same tokeniser, and thirteen
times for Malayalam. So either my sentence is unusual, or my metric differs
from theirs, or the number I was told is old. I do not know which yet, and I
would rather say that than pick the flattering answer.

clip

Last thing, and this is the one that matters.

None of this is because Devanagari or Tamil script is somehow harder for a
computer. I ran the same sentences through a newer tokeniser. The cost went
down.

Nobody decided your language should be expensive. Somebody decided what text
to train the tokeniser on, and the cost followed that decision. Which means
it is fixable. That is a different feeling from "my script is expensive", and
it is the accurate one.

Tell me what I lost you on.
