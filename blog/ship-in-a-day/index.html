Ship in a day: how Guldaar went from idea to live site in 24 hours

Jains · AI & Digital Transformation Agency

Most website projects take three months. Almost none of that is building. It's waiting — for copy, for a logo decision, for the third round of feedback on a colour, for someone to find the photos.

We ran Guldaar Bazaar, a South Asian clothing and jewellery store selling into Europe, from empty domain to live storefront inside a day. Then we did it again for Guldaar Basera, a Himalayan homestay in Ranikhet. Here's exactly how, because the method transfers to almost any small business — and because the interesting part isn't speed, it's what speed forces you to leave out.

The premise: a live site beats a good plan

A site that exists collects real signal — what people click, what they ask about, what they abandon. A site in planning collects opinions. Twelve weeks of opinions will produce a worse site than one week of traffic, every time.

Launch is not the end of the design process. It's the first day you have any evidence.

So the goal on day one isn't the final site. It's the earliest possible version that a real customer could complete a real transaction on. Everything else waits for data.

The 24 hours, hour by hour

Hours 0–2 — one conversation, and only one. Not a discovery phase. One call that answers four questions: who buys this, what do they need to see before they trust you, what's the single action we want them to take, and what do you already have — photos, product list, prices. We record it. That recording is the brief; nobody writes a separate document.

Hours 2–4 — assets, ruthlessly. Whatever exists already, we use. Phone photos of the actual products beat stock imagery for a business like this, and they beat waiting three weeks for a shoot. Where something is genuinely missing, AI fills the gap for launch and gets replaced later — placeholder is a state, not a defect.

Hours 4–10 — copy and structure together. This is where most projects lose a month, and where AI actually earns its place. We draft every page from the call recording, then edit hard for voice. The draft isn't the output — it removes the blank page, which is the thing that costs weeks. A human rewrites every sentence that a customer will make a decision on.

Hours 10–18 — build. No CMS unless the client will genuinely edit weekly. No framework a small business can't get help with later. Static pages, a proper checkout or booking flow, real product data. Fast by construction, because there's nothing in it that isn't needed.

Hours 18–22 — mobile, speed, payments. Nearly all of this audience arrives on a phone over an uneven connection. We test on a real device on mobile data, not a desktop browser resized. Then we put a real card through checkout. A store that hasn't taken one live payment hasn't launched.

Hours 22–24 — domain, analytics, handover. DNS, HTTPS, a search console, and a twenty-minute walkthrough recorded for the owner. They leave with the keys.

What we deliberately did not build

  • A customer login. Nobody creates an account before their first purchase. Guest checkout only.
  • A blog. An empty blog with three stale posts damages trust more than no blog at all.
  • A wishlist, a loyalty scheme, a recommendation engine. Every one of these is a feature for a store that already has customers.
  • Multi-currency and multi-language. Added later, when the analytics said where people were actually arriving from — which was not where anyone guessed.
  • A logo debate. Wordmark in a good typeface on day one. It's still there.

Each of those would have added a week. Together they'd have added the three months we were trying to avoid — and the store would have learned nothing in the meantime.

Where AI actually helped, and where it didn't

It helped with first-draft copy for dozens of product descriptions, with alt text and metadata, with turning a rambling call recording into a clean structure, and with generating the placeholder imagery that let us ship without waiting on a photographer.

It didn't help with any decision that required knowing the customer. Which products go on the homepage, what objection to answer first, how to price for a European buyer purchasing from India — those came from the person who's run the business. AI removes the blank page. It doesn't remove the judgement.

Run this on your own business

You can do this yourself. The compressed version:

  • Pick one action you want a visitor to take. Delete every element that doesn't lead to it.
  • Use the assets you have today. Replace them after launch, with evidence.
  • Write the copy from a recording of yourself explaining the business to a stranger. It's better than anything you'll type.
  • Set a hard deadline of one day and treat every "but we should also…" as a note for week two.
  • Put a real payment or a real booking through before you tell anyone it's live.

The constraint is the method. A week-long deadline produces a week's worth of second-guessing; a one-day deadline produces a live site.
