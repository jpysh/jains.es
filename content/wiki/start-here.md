I am learning to build language models from scratch, and writing down what I
work out on the day I work it out.

I am not a researcher. I spent years as a product manager, I know what these
systems do at the level a product manager knows it, and I had never written a
training loop. The maths is rusty. That is the starting position, and the whole
point of doing this in public is that you can watch it change.

## Why this exists

Most explanations of how language models work are written by people who already
understood them. They skip the part where you are confused, because they cannot
remember being confused. I am writing while confused, which is a short window
and worth using.

The audience I am writing for is mine: people in India, across the Global South,
and anyone for whom English is a second or third language. Where a topic has a
cost, a language or an access angle, that is the angle I take, because that is
the part nobody else writes.

## How to read a page

Every page says how finished it is, at the top.

- **Seedling** — rough, written the day I learnt it, and likely wrong in
  places. Most pages start here and many stay here.
- **Budding** — checked and revised, still growing.
- **Evergreen** — audited against its sources, every citation opened and
  confirmed. Only a small number of pages ever earn this.

A seedling is not an apology. It is the honest label on work that is still
moving, and it is what makes publishing daily possible at all. If a page had to
be finished before it could exist, nothing would exist.

## If you find a mistake

Tell me. Every page has an **Edit this page** link at the foot that opens the
source file on GitHub, and every newsletter ends with an invitation to reply.

Corrections get an explanation, not a silent edit — what was wrong, how it got
through, and how it was caught. A bare correction is worse than none, because
it tells you the page changed without telling you why you should trust the new
version.

## Where to go next

Start with [tokenisation and the cost of your language](/wiki/tokenisation-and-the-cost-of-your-language/).
It is the first topic page here, it needs no maths, and it answers a question
with a number: why the same sentence costs about eight times more in Hindi
than in English when you call a model's API.

Beyond that there is not much here yet. The wiki fills up one day at a time,
and the [daily log](/day/) is the front of it — that is where each session lands first,
including the sessions that did not work.

The curriculum for the next twelve weeks is in the repository, and so is
everything else: the prompts, the sources, the plan and its earlier draft. None
of it is hidden, because the method is as much the point as the result.
