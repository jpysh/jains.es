The Department of Homeland Security published a proposed rule on 11 September 2026 that would eliminate the discretionary 60-day grace period allowing H-1B and similar nonimmigrant visa holders to remain in the US after their employment ends. The Federal Register notice describes the current evaluation process as a burden that is "time consuming and complex." Comments close on 10 November 2026, according to the notice, and HR Dive's Kathryn Moody reported the proposal the same day it published.

Nothing has changed yet. A proposed rule is a proposal: it must go through the comment period, and DHS must then decide whether to finalise it, modify it, or drop it. The 60-day grace period remains available today. But if it is finalised, the operational cost lands on the employer's termination process, not on USCIS.

## What the grace period currently absorbs

The 60 days after employment ends is the window in which a visa-holding employee finds a new sponsoring employer, files a change of status, or arranges to leave. It is also, invisibly, the window that makes ordinary HR mechanics work for this population.

Today a termination for an H-1B holder looks broadly like a termination for anyone else. Notice is given, a last day is set, the departure is processed, and the immigration consequence is handled after the fact, usually by an external immigration firm the employee also deals with. HR does not have to model the timeline because the timeline has 60 days of slack in it.

Remove that slack and the employee's status becomes a function of the date your process sets. The same restructuring, run the same way, produces a materially different outcome for a visa-holding employee than for a colleague sitting next to them.

## Where it would actually bite

**Restructuring and reduction timelines.** A redundancy programme typically fixes an exit date for a population and works backwards. If lawful presence ends with employment, the exit date is the whole question for part of that population, and it is being set by a process optimised for something else.

**Notice and garden leave.** Whether notice is worked, paid in lieu, or spent on leave changes when employment legally ends, and that distinction has consequences here that it does not have for other staff. Payment in lieu of notice, which is often the administratively convenient option, may be the worst one for an affected employee.

**Transfer windows.** Employees moving to a new sponsoring employer rely on the gap. Without it, the mechanics of a transfer have to complete before the last day rather than after it, which affects how much notice you can give and whether you can enforce a confidentiality period before a departure is known.

**Performance exits and summary dismissals.** An immediate termination for cause is a different act when it also ends lawful presence. Counsel will want to be involved in cases that currently do not reach them.

**Anything you outsource.** Most HR teams manage this population through an immigration vendor and rarely model it directly. If your vendor is the only party who understands the timeline, your termination process cannot account for it, because the people who set dates are not the people who know what the dates do.

## The comment period is the leverage

Comments close 10 November 2026. If this rule would change how your organisation runs terminations or restructures, that is the window in which the operational cost can be put on the record, and it is the only part of this you can act on before the outcome is decided.

A comment that describes a specific mechanic — how your notice periods work, how a reduction timeline is built, what a transfer actually requires — is more useful to the record than a statement of position. DHS's stated rationale is administrative burden on its own evaluation process. The concrete counterweight is the burden shifted onto employers, and only employers can describe that.

Decide whether you are filing, and if so who drafts it: counsel, your immigration vendor, an industry body, or your own people operations team. Any of those is fine. Deciding in late October is not, because the drafting and internal approval will not fit.

## What to do before November

**Count the exposure.** How many employees are on H-1B or comparable status, in which teams, and how many of those teams are in scope for any restructuring currently being planned. This number is usually held by the immigration vendor rather than in your HRIS reporting.

**Walk one termination end to end with counsel.** Take a real recent case, assume no grace period, and identify every date your process sets and what it would have caused. This is a one-hour exercise that produces the whole picture.

**Ask counsel the questions rather than reading templates.** What would change about how we set exit dates. Whether notice paid in lieu is materially worse for affected employees than notice worked. What we can and cannot say to an affected employee, given that we are not their immigration advisers. Where the discrimination risk sits if we start treating this population differently in a restructuring — which is a real risk that runs in the opposite direction from the accommodation instinct.

**Tell the people who set dates.** Whoever builds your restructuring timelines needs to know that one input has consequences it did not have before. That is a briefing, not a policy change, and it can happen now.

The same proposal reads differently if you are hiring into a shortage rather than restructuring out of one. Employers competing for [data centre and infrastructure skills](/blog/data-centre-talent-shortage/) are drawing from a pool where visa status is already a live constraint on who will accept an offer.

None of this requires the rule to be finalised. Most of it is worth doing regardless, because it surfaces how little most HR functions know about a population they are already responsible for.
