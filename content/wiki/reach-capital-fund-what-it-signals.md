Reach Capital closed an oversubscribed $265m Fund V in under six months, TechCrunch reported on 18 August, writing cheques of $1m to $10m from pre-seed to Series A into roughly 50 companies over three years, aimed at AI applications in learning, work and health. The number that matters to an L&D buyer is not the fund size. It is the stage. Fifty new companies at pre-seed to Series A, funded specifically to build learning and work tools, will produce a steady supply of vendors pitching you products with fewer than twenty employees behind them.

The fund is up from $215m in 2023 and $165m in 2021. TechCrunch noted the context that established firms took over 90% of the roughly $62bn raised across US venture funds through May 2026. A specialist raising a larger fund quickly, while the overall edtech funding total contracts, tells you the shape of the supply side for the next three years: fewer dollars in the category overall, and a meaningful share of them going to companies at the earliest stage.

## Why that combination is awkward for a buyer

A pre-Series-A vendor is usually where the genuinely new product is. It is also a company with one year of runway, no security team, a founder answering support tickets, and a roadmap that will change when their next investor asks it to. Both things are true at once, and the standard procurement answer of refusing to buy from companies under a certain size means you see the new capability two years after your competitors.

The awkwardness gets worse because of how these tools arrive. They do not come through procurement. An enthusiastic internal sponsor discovers something, runs it on a corporate card, and by the time L&D hears about it there is employee data in the system and a champion who will take a refusal personally. Deciding your position under that pressure is how organisations end up with either a blanket ban nobody follows or a pilot with no boundaries.

## Write the policy now

Four decisions, made once, applied to every early-stage learning vendor. The point is to have the answer before the pitch, not to be strict.

**Pilot budget and duration, fixed in advance.** A number you will spend to find out whether something works, and a hard end date. If it works you renegotiate properly with procurement; if it does not you stop. A pilot without an end date is a procurement decision made by default.

**Data boundaries by category, not by vendor.** Decide which classes of data may enter an unproven system: anonymised usage, yes; named performance or assessment records, no; anything feeding a promotion, pay or disciplinary decision, no, regardless of how good the product is. Vendor-by-vendor judgement produces inconsistency you cannot defend when someone asks why one team's data went somewhere another team's did not.

**An exit plan written before the start.** What you do if the company folds, is acquired, or pivots mid-pilot. For an early-stage vendor this is a realistic outcome rather than a hypothetical, so the answer needs to be concrete: whose data is in there, how it comes out, what the team falls back to, and who owns executing that.

**A named internal owner who is not the sponsor.** The person who found the tool is the wrong person to evaluate it. Someone in L&D or ops owns the assessment against a criterion agreed at the start.

## What to be less worried about than you probably are

Company size on its own is a poor predictor of whether a learning tool will work for you. A twelve-person company that has solved one narrow problem well is frequently a better bet for a specific workflow than a platform whose feature covering your need was built to close a deal and has not been touched since.

Security posture is the legitimate concern, and it is checkable rather than a matter of judgement. Ask for their subprocessor list, their data retention policy, where the data sits, and whether employee records are used for model training. A serious early-stage company answers those in a day. One that cannot has told you what you needed to know, and the answer is unrelated to headcount.

## The three-year read

Fifty new companies from one fund, against an overall category total that fell 26% in the first half of 2026 according to HolonIQ's July figures, which [we looked at separately](/blog/edtech-funding-fell-what-it-means/). More vendors, less money each, therefore more failures and more acquisitions. As a buyer, that means running short pilots with clean exits should be your normal operating mode for the next three years rather than an exception you handle case by case.

Do that and early-stage vendors become cheap to try. Fail to do it and every one of them becomes a negotiation you conduct under pressure from someone who has already fallen in love with the product.
