Employees who spoke negatively about their employer's AI rated leadership 2.04 out of 5, against 3.34 from employees who praised it — the widest gap of any category measured in an analysis of more than 500,000 Glassdoor reviews by the AI-Driven Enterprise Institute, reported by HR Dive's Lara Ewen on 8 September 2026. The breakdown by company behaviour is the part worth taking to your communications team: employers the researchers classified as "AI visionaries", high on talk and low on execution, drew 27.5% negative reviews. Companies with balanced talk and delivery drew 12.6%. Quiet "stealth adopters" drew 7.2%.

Announcing an AI strategy you have not shipped costs you almost four times the negative sentiment of shipping without announcing.

## Why AI opens the widest gap of any topic

Employees rate leadership on plenty of things, and the research found AI sentiment produced the largest spread of the categories measured. A 1.3-point swing in leadership rating on a five-point scale is not a rounding difference.

The likely mechanism is that AI announcements make a specific, testable promise about the employee's own working day. A statement about becoming an AI-first organisation is heard as: your work is about to change, and someone has decided how. That is immediately falsifiable, unlike most strategic messaging, because the employee finds out within weeks whether anything arrived.

When nothing arrives, the employee has learned something concrete about leadership — not that AI failed, but that leadership announces things that do not happen. That generalises to everything else leadership says, which is why the penalty lands on the leadership rating rather than on a technology rating.

## The stealth number is the uncomfortable one

Quiet adopters at 7.2% negative is lower than the balanced group at 12.6%. Read narrowly, the data says that employees are least unhappy when AI adoption happens without being communicated as a programme at all.

That should not be read as advice to hide what you are doing. Employees who discover that a hiring, scheduling or performance tool involved AI they were never told about have a legitimate grievance, and several regulatory regimes now require that disclosure. It also breaks in exactly the way the visionary category breaks, only later and worse.

The defensible reading is narrower: the announcement layer adds risk and no benefit. The difference between 7.2% and 27.5% is not caused by whether AI is deployed. It is caused by the distance between what was claimed and what employees can see working. Stealth adopters score well because that distance is zero by construction.

## The practical rule

Tie every external AI claim to something an employee can see working.

That is a sequencing rule, and it inverts the usual order. Most AI communications plans start with a vision statement, run an internal launch, then deliver capability over the following year. The Glassdoor data suggests running it the other way: deliver one thing to one team, let it work, then describe what exists.

The test for any draft announcement, all-hands slide or earnings line: can a named employee open something today that does what this sentence claims? If not, the sentence is a forecast. Forecasts are fine in a strategy document that employees do not read as a promise about next month, and they are expensive in an all-hands.

This also disciplines the upward communication. An AI programme reporting adoption percentages to a board, with nothing an employee can point at, is generating the visionary profile deliberately. We wrote about [the rework hiding inside AI productivity claims](/wiki/half-of-ai-time-is-rework/) — the same gap between the claim and the experience shows up there, in the numbers rather than in the announcements.

## What HR and internal communications should change

**Stop approving AI messaging that has no shipped referent.** Give communications a standing rule: every AI claim names a tool, a team using it, and what it does. If that cannot be filled in, the claim waits.

**Separate the ambition document from the employee-facing message.** Investors and candidates can be told where you are going. Employees should be told what has changed and what changes next month, because that is the only part they can verify.

**Watch your own leadership scores against AI mentions.** If you run an engagement survey with free text, or if you read your Glassdoor reviews, count how many AI mentions are about something promised versus something in use. That ratio is the say-do gap in your own data, and it is cheaper to measure than a new survey.

**Ship narrow.** One team, one workflow, something that works this month. The balanced group at 12.6% did not get there by delivering a platform. They got there by not being more than one step ahead of what exists.

**Say when something did not work.** The visionary penalty is about distance between claim and reality. Closing it by retracting a claim costs less than leaving it open, and employees already know.

The awkward implication for anyone who has already announced a large AI programme is that you are currently carrying the visionary discount and cannot un-announce it. The only available move is to close the distance from the delivery side, one visible thing at a time, and to stop adding to the claim while you do.
