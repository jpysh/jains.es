Fifty-five per cent of workers regularly use AI, but only one in three received employer-provided AI training in the past six months, and that training concentrated on AI literacy and basic prompting, according to research The Conference Board published on 28 July, covered by HR Dive on 31 July. The survey covered nearly 1,300 workers. The finding that should move budget is this: the research reports that workers who direct AI agents outperform those who only prompt, while advanced skills such as managing AI agents are underemphasised in what employers teach. Principal researcher Matt Rosenbaum put it plainly, saying that AI literacy alone will not create business value.

If your AI curriculum this year is a prompting course and an acceptable-use module, you have trained the level below the one where the measured performance difference appears.

## What the distinction actually is

Prompting is a single exchange. A person asks, the model answers, the person judges the answer and uses it or discards it. The skill involved is phrasing and evaluation, and it takes a competent adult a few hours to get most of the available benefit.

Directing agents is running a process. A person decomposes work into steps, hands the steps to a system that will execute several of them without checking back, decides what the system is allowed to touch, specifies what finished looks like, and reviews output they did not watch being produced. The skills are task decomposition, scoping, verification and knowing which parts must stay with a human. Those are supervisory skills, and they are closer to managing a junior colleague than to using a search engine.

That is why the performance gap exists, and it is also why a prompting course does not close it. The two activities share an interface and almost nothing else.

## What to move budget to

**Verification practice, with real work.** The core competency in agent supervision is checking output you did not produce. Build sessions around actual artefacts from the team's own work: here is what the system produced, find what is wrong with it, say how you know. This is trainable, it is assessable, and almost nobody is teaching it.

**Task decomposition on live processes.** Take a real end-to-end process the team owns. Have them mark which steps an agent can run unsupervised, which need a checkpoint, and which must stay human. Then run it and compare what they predicted against what happened. The disagreements are the curriculum.

**Scoping and permissions.** What the agent is allowed to read, write, send and delete. Most organisations treat this as an IT configuration question. It is a judgement your staff make several times a day, and they are making it now without having been taught anything about it.

**Review at handover.** When an agent produces something a person signs their name to, the signer needs a habit for establishing they can account for it. This connects directly to a separate finding that 29% of workers have delivered work they could not explain, covered in [our piece on learning debt](/blog/learning-debt/). The same training investment addresses both.

## The part that decides whether any of it works

The Conference Board's barrier finding is the one to argue in front of your executive team: protected time and manager support determine whether the training converts. Without both, you have bought a completion statistic.

Protected time means hours in the working week where practising is the job, blocked and defended. Not lunchtime, not a webinar people join with their inbox open. Agent supervision is a practical skill and practical skills need reps against real work, which means the reps take capacity away from delivery for a period. That is the cost. Executive sponsorship of AI training that does not include releasing capacity is sponsorship of the announcement.

Manager support means the direct manager can do the thing, will review it, and does not treat time spent learning as slack. A manager who has had no training themselves cannot supervise supervision, which makes managers the first cohort rather than a later one.

## Stop counting adoption

The usual board metric for AI is usage: licences activated, weekly active users, prompts per head. Every one of those numbers goes up when people use AI as a shortcut and up when they use it well, so it cannot distinguish the two, and it rises fastest in exactly the population that has had no training.

Replace it with something that separates them. Two candidates that are cheap to collect: the proportion of a team's agent-run processes where a named human can state the checkpoints and why they are there, and the rework rate on AI-assisted output at review. Both go down when people get better. Neither can be gamed by opening a chat window more often.

## A reasonable next quarter

Audit what your current AI curriculum teaches against the two levels described above, which takes a morning and usually produces an uncomfortable answer. Pick one team with a real process, train managers first, run decomposition and verification against that process with protected hours, and measure rework at review before and after. If the rework rate does not move, you have learned something real for the cost of one team's time, which is a better outcome than rolling out prompting training to everyone and reporting the completion figure.
