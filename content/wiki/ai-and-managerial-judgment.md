Managers who lean too heavily on generative AI lose knowledge-related capabilities that are normally built through experience — contextual understanding, moral insight, job knowledge — according to a [paper in Academy of Management Review](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amr.2024.0582) by researchers at US, UK and Swiss universities, led by Dirk Lindebaum of the University of Bath, reported by HR Dive's Ryan Golden on 9 September 2026. The authors find the risk highest among time-pressured managers using AI as a shortcut, and propose "epistemic up-skilling": using AI to challenge a manager's assumptions rather than to produce their decisions.

Which means the metric most organisations report to their boards — AI usage volume — rewards precisely the behaviour the paper identifies as harmful.

## What the paper actually claims

The argument is about how managerial knowledge is acquired, not about whether AI output is accurate. A manager develops contextual judgement by doing the work: sitting with an ambiguous performance case, weighing a decision that has no clean answer, being wrong and finding out why. That accumulation is the asset. The paper's case is that delegating the reasoning to a model removes the experience that would have built it.

Two qualifications worth keeping straight. This is conceptual work in a theory journal, not a measured effect in a population of managers — the authors are advancing an argument about mechanism. And the claim is about overreliance under time pressure, not about use. The authors propose a different use, not abstinence.

Both qualifications make it more usable, not less. You do not need to wait for longitudinal evidence to stop rewarding a behaviour the argument identifies as the harmful one, because usage volume was never a good metric anyway.

## Why the adoption metric is the problem

Almost every enterprise AI programme reports some version of the same figures: licences deployed, weekly active users, percentage of staff using the tool, queries per head. These exist because they are the numbers the vendor's dashboard produces.

They measure quantity of delegation. A manager who sends every ambiguous decision to a model scores extremely well. A manager who writes out their own reasoning, asks the model to attack it, and changes their mind on one point in five scores lower on volume and is doing the thing the paper recommends.

That is not a subtle distortion. It is the metric pointing at the wrong behaviour with money and visibility attached, in the population where the consequences compound — because a manager whose judgement stops developing keeps getting promoted on the strength of the judgement they had.

## The usable instruction

The paper's proposal converts into something you can put in a manager guideline: require AI to be used against a manager's stated reasoning, not in place of it.

In practice that means a sequence. The manager writes their own position first — what they think should happen and why. Then they ask the model for the strongest case against it, the factors they have not considered, and what someone who disagreed would say. Then they decide, and record what changed.

Writing the position first is what does the work. It forces the reasoning to exist before the model can supply a substitute, and it produces a record of a human decision rather than an accepted output. That record is also what you want if the decision is ever examined, which is increasingly likely in employment matters — HR Dive reported on 23 June 2026 that FEHA claims over Workday's AI screening tools survived a motion to dismiss, and we looked at [what that means for vendor selection](/wiki/workday-ai-hiring-ruling-california/) separately.

The sequence costs the manager more time than pasting the case into a chat box. That is the point, and it is why the time-pressured manager is the one the paper flags. A guideline that does not come with relief on the time pressure will be ignored by exactly the population it is aimed at.

## What to change this quarter

**Retire usage volume as an adoption measure for managers.** Keep it for licence management if finance needs it. Take it out of the programme scorecard and out of anything a manager is assessed on.

**Replace it with something about the decision.** Whether the manager's own position was recorded before the AI was consulted. Whether the AI was asked for a counter-case. How often the manager's initial view changed. These are observable in the artefacts managers already produce if you ask for the position in writing, and they are harder to game than query counts because gaming them requires doing the reasoning.

**Write the guideline for the decisions that matter most.** Performance ratings, promotion cases, disciplinary matters, redundancy selection, hiring rejections. Do not attempt to govern every use of AI by every manager. Govern the decisions that affect someone's employment.

**Give the time back.** If a manager has twenty minutes for a performance case, they will take the shortcut regardless of the guideline. Either the calendar changes or the guideline is decoration.

**Check what your development programmes now assume.** If your management development relies on managers accumulating judgement through repeated exposure to hard cases, and AI is absorbing those cases, the programme is running on a supply of experience that is being quietly redirected.

The item most likely to be resisted is the first one, because usage volume is the only AI number many programmes currently have, and removing it leaves the board asking what replaced it. The answer — evidence that managers are reasoning rather than delegating — is a better answer, and it takes a quarter to be able to give it.
