Kelly Services published its Data Center Salary Guide on 25 August 2026, reporting roughly 650,000 expected data centre positions — up 30% on 2023 — plus more than 180,000 related construction jobs through 2028. Ninety per cent of operators told Kelly staffing shortages are a critical constraint, and 25% of data centre personnel are being poached by competing hyperscalers. The guide's national midpoints include $175,000 for AI engineers and $178,000 for data centre operators, with 46 percentage points of variation between regions, as reported by HR Dive's Laurel Kalser on 3 September 2026.

If you employ electricians, mechanical technicians, HVAC specialists, security operations staff or facilities managers, those midpoints are now your competition, whether or not you consider yourself a technology employer.

## The constraint moved from power to people

The public story about AI infrastructure has been about electricity — grid connections, turbine lead times, siting fights. Kelly's finding is that staffing has become the binding constraint for the operators themselves: 90% calling it critical is close to unanimity in survey terms.

That matters to employers outside the sector because a constraint of that size gets solved with money, and the money is spent on people who already have jobs somewhere else. The 25% poaching figure Kelly reports is hyperscalers taking from each other. The pool underneath that is the general industrial, commercial and facilities workforce, which is where your building maintenance team lives.

## The roles at risk are not the ones on your watchlist

Most talent teams have a scarcity list, and it is populated by engineers, data scientists and whatever the current AI title is. Those roles get quarterly benchmarking, retention bonuses and a named recruiter.

The roles Kelly is describing sit in a different part of the organisation and a different part of the budget. A high-voltage electrician, a critical-environment technician, a building management systems specialist, a night-shift operations controller. These are typically benchmarked against local trade rates on an annual cycle, managed by facilities rather than by talent acquisition, and often filled through a staffing agency whose rate card is treated as the market.

That rate card is lagging. A $178,000 national midpoint for a data centre operator, with 46 points of regional variation, means that in the metros where data centre construction is concentrated the local rate for adjacent skills has moved sharply, and an annual benchmark taken from last year's placements will not show it. The first signal you get will be a resignation from someone you assumed was not going anywhere.

## Where the replacements actually come from

Kelly's recommendation, per HR Dive's report, is to recruit from telecoms and utilities. That is the useful part of the guide, because it points at a different pool rather than a higher bid in the same one.

Telecoms and utilities produce people who already hold the things that are genuinely hard to teach: working safely around energised equipment, comfort with shift-based critical operations, discipline about procedure and logging, tolerance for being the person who gets called at 3am. The gap between those workers and a data centre role is mostly equipment familiarity, which is trainable in months.

For an employer outside the data centre sector, the same logic runs in reverse and slightly cheaper. You are not competing on the $178,000 midpoint for every role. You are competing for people whose transferable skill is operational discipline, and you can recruit them from the same adjacent industries before the hyperscalers get further into that pool.

## Four things to do this quarter

**Re-benchmark the unglamorous roles against data centre midpoints, not local trade rates.** Pick your electrical, mechanical, critical-environment and facilities-operations roles and price them against what Kelly reports for the metros you operate in. Expect to find gaps that your annual survey did not surface, because the survey measured last year.

**Find out whether a data centre is being built near each of your sites.** Construction alone draws 180,000-plus jobs through 2028 on Kelly's numbers, and construction hiring hits your trades before operations hiring does. Site-level exposure varies enormously, and a national average will conceal it.

**Move these roles onto the scarcity list formally.** That means the same treatment your engineering roles get: quarterly benchmarking, a named owner, a retention conversation with each incumbent rather than an exit interview after the fact. This is a governance change more than a budget one, and it is the step most likely to be skipped because nobody currently owns it.

**Open a recruiting channel into telecoms and utilities.** Write the job descriptions around operational competence rather than sector experience, and set a training budget for equipment familiarity. If your job postings require data centre experience, you are advertising exclusively into the pool everyone else is bidding in.

## The part that will be tempting and wrong

The obvious response to a 46-point regional spread is to move the work to a cheaper region. For genuinely remote work that can be right. For the roles under discussion here it is not available, because the equipment is where the building is.

The other supply-side variable is who is allowed to stay. [DHS's proposal to end the 60-day H-1B grace period](/blog/h1b-grace-period/) would change the risk an international candidate carries in accepting a role, in a market where those candidates are already being competed over.

The second tempting move is to wait for the shortage to correct. Kelly's numbers describe demand running through 2028 against a pool that takes years to expand, since you cannot train a high-voltage electrician in a quarter. Waiting means paying the 2028 rate for someone you could have retained at this year's rate with a conversation.
