Edtech companies raised $1bn in the first half of 2026, down 26% from $1.35bn in the same period of 2025, according to HolonIQ's funding note published on 14 July. Deal volume stayed roughly flat. The same number of companies took a quarter less money between them, which means the average cheque shrank, which means the average venture-backed learning vendor on your renewal list has less runway than it did a year ago. That is the part that lands on an L&D budget holder, and it lands at renewal, not at some future funding announcement.

## What a flat deal count on a smaller pot actually does

A funding total falling while deal count holds is not a story about investor mood. It is a story about how long your suppliers can operate before they need to raise again. A company that took $6m instead of $9m on the same team size and the same burn has moved its next raise forward by months. Some of those companies will raise. Some will sell. Some will cut the parts of the product that cost most to maintain and serve fewest customers, and integrations are usually top of that list.

HolonIQ reported that workforce training and development remained the largest recipient category. That is your category. It is where the money still is, which is good news for the supply of new tools, and it means the thinning is happening inside the segment you buy from rather than somewhere adjacent.

Two other details from the note are worth holding. East Asian deal volume rose 37%, so more of the new entrants pitching you will be based outside your usual procurement comfort zone. And HolonIQ reported continued investor interest in alternative credentials and work-integrated learning, which tells you where product roadmaps are being pointed for the next three years.

## The renewal questions this changes

Most L&D renewal diligence covers price, seat count, uptime and content refresh. None of that tells you whether the vendor will exist in the shape you bought in three years. Add four things.

**Ask when they last raised and how much.** Not as a gotcha. A vendor with two years of runway will answer it. One that deflects has told you the answer. You are not judging their business, you are sizing how much of your learning record is sitting somewhere that may change ownership inside your contract term.

**Get data portability in writing, at field level.** "You can export your data" is not a commitment. Completion records, assessment scores, competency mappings, manager sign-offs and the timestamps on all of it, in a documented format, on demand, without a professional services engagement. If your compliance training evidence lives only in a vendor's database and that vendor is acquired, you will find out how good the export is at the worst possible moment.

**Price the multi-year discount honestly.** A three-year commitment at 20% off is a good deal from a stable vendor and an expensive bet on a thinly funded one. The discount is the vendor buying certainty from you. Decide whether you are the right party to sell it.

**Name the acquisition scenario in the contract.** Change of control clauses are standard in enterprise software and routinely absent from learning platform deals signed by L&D teams without procurement in the room. At minimum: notice, a termination right, and continuity of the data export obligation through the transition.

## What not to do with this number

Do not read a 26% fall as a reason to freeze vendor selection and stay on the incumbent. The incumbent is subject to the same funding environment, and inertia is not diligence. The number is not an argument for buying less learning technology. It is an argument for buying it with different questions attached.

Also resist the temptation to treat the funding market as a proxy for product quality. Cheque size correlates with a founder's ability to raise, not with whether the tool moves a completion rate. Some of the best-run learning vendors have never taken a large round, and some of the worst took the largest.

## The three-year read

If deal count stays flat while totals fall, the market is producing the same number of companies with less money each. That produces two things over three years: more acquisitions, as thinly funded companies become cheaper to buy than to fund, and more feature consolidation, as the survivors absorb catalogues rather than build. For a buyer, that means the vendor you sign with today is more likely to become a product line inside something larger than it was a year ago.

Plan for it rather than against it. Keep your learning record extractable, keep contract terms shorter than your confidence in the vendor, and treat any single-vendor dependency on assessment or credential data as a risk that needs an owner. If you are running a pilot with a venture-backed platform this half, decide now what happens to that data if the company is bought before the pilot ends. The answer takes an afternoon to negotiate before you sign and is unavailable afterwards.

The falling total does not mean fewer vendors will contact you. [Reach Capital's $265m fund](/wiki/reach-capital-fund-what-it-signals/) is aimed at pre-seed and Series A companies in exactly this category, which means the pipeline of very small vendors pitching learning products is being refilled while the money per company falls.

The related question is what kind of vendor will be pitching you as this plays out, which is a different story with a different set of numbers behind it.
