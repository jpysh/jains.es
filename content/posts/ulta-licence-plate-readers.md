Ulta Beauty confirmed it had deployed Flock Safety automated licence plate readers, without facial recognition, at under 1% of its roughly 1,500 stores to address organised retail crime, after which the activist group UltraViolet ran a campaign that filled Ulta's Instagram comments with complaints, according to Retail Dive's report on 4 September 2026. The useful detail for loss prevention leaders is the arithmetic. Fewer than fifteen stores carried the technology, which is nowhere near enough scope to have returned its cost, and the reputational exposure landed across the whole brand.

That asymmetry is the lesson, and it is specific to surveillance technology. A pilot of a new planogram, a new POS or a new fulfilment method at under 1% of stores is genuinely contained; if it fails, it fails in fifteen stores. A surveillance pilot is contained operationally and unbounded reputationally, because the story is the decision to use the technology rather than the number of car parks it runs in.

## Why the footprint does not limit the downside

Three things travel beyond the pilot stores.

**The policy is company-wide even when the hardware is not.** Once a retailer has confirmed it uses plate readers anywhere, the question every customer asks is whether their store is one of them. The answer requires a list, and publishing a list of surveilled locations is its own decision.

**Plate readers are a recognised category with existing opponents.** Flock's technology has an established public profile and organised critics who were already paying attention. A retailer adopting it inherits an argument that has been running for years and did not start with them. Ulta's clarification that no facial recognition was involved is accurate and did not change the shape of the response, because the objection is to vehicle tracking, not to face matching.

**The audience is concentrated.** Beauty retail runs on social channels with high engagement, which means the same asset that drives discovery is the channel a campaign uses. A category with a quieter social presence would absorb this differently.

## The sequencing that avoids it

The failure here is not the technology choice. It is that the customer communications position arrived after the deployment and after the complaints, which means it arrived as a defence.

Before hardware is installed anywhere:

- **Write the public answer first.** One paragraph saying what is collected, why, how long it is kept, who it is shared with, and whether it reaches law enforcement. If nobody will sign that paragraph, the programme is not ready regardless of the loss-prevention case.
- **Get sign-off from marketing and communications, not just legal and LP.** Legal review answers whether you may. It does not answer what happens to the brand, and the people who own the brand are usually told after the contract is signed.
- **Decide the data retention and sharing terms before procurement, not in the vendor's default configuration.** Retention period and third-party access are the two questions every critic asks, and a vendor default is not a position you can defend as your own.
- **Assume signage-level disclosure becomes the story.** In-lot signage that discloses the technology is both a likely legal requirement and a public artefact that will be photographed. Write it as though it will be.
- **Score the pilot on stores where the crime data justifies it and accept that this is now a brand decision.** If the shrink concentration does not support a footprint large enough to matter, the honest conclusion may be that the technology is not the right intervention for you, not that you should run it small.

## The trade-off is real, which is why it needs a number

Organised retail crime is a genuine cost and beauty is one of the categories hit hardest. Nobody in loss prevention is choosing surveillance for entertainment. The problem with the Ulta sequence is that it spent the brand cost without having established the operational benefit, because a sub-1% deployment cannot establish one yet.

The version that works, if any version does, requires both halves quantified before launch: the shrink reduction you expect from the specific stores, and the customer-communications position you are prepared to defend publicly. That second half is the same discipline the [FTC's personalized pricing proposal](/blog/ftc-personalized-pricing/) is pushing onto pricing teams: be able to say plainly what you do with customer data before someone else characterises it for you. Loss prevention leaders who bring only the first number to the committee are the reason the second one gets decided by an activist campaign.

For anyone reviewing a surveillance proposal in the next quarter, the practical filter is this. Ask whether you would launch it with a press release. If the plan depends on nobody noticing, the plan has already failed, because a retailer with 1,500 stores does not get to deploy anything unnoticed.
