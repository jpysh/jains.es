The EU AI Act obligations covering AI used in employment did not start applying on 2 August 2026. Following the Digital Omnibus on AI, the European Commission's own [implementation timeline](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/timeline/timeline-implementation-eu-ai-act) now shows the Annex III high-risk rules — which cover hiring, promotion, task allocation and dismissal — applying from 2 December 2027, with AI embedded in regulated products following on 2 August 2028. The European Parliament's press office confirmed MEPs supported the postponement in its statement of 18 March 2026. The driver, per the Commission's [FAQ on the Digital Omnibus](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/faq), was that the harmonised standards from CEN/CENELEC were not ready.

Sixteen extra months is a scheduling change. It is not a repeal, a carve-out, or a signal that the obligations will be softened when they arrive.

## What is still true

The Act is in force. Annex III still classifies employment uses of AI as high risk. The obligations attached to that classification — risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, transparency to affected people, registration — still exist in the text. What changed is the date on which they become enforceable against deployers and providers of those systems.

Anyone telling you the AI Act has been watered down for HR should be asked to point at the provision. The Commission's timeline page is short and public; read it rather than a law firm's summary of it.

## What actually happens when a deadline slips

Governance programmes are usually funded as deadline projects. A date exists, a budget is approved against it, a working group meets, and the work is scoped to finish just before the date. Remove the date and the budget becomes the easiest line to cut in the next planning round, because nothing breaks this quarter if you cut it.

That is the failure mode here, and it is expensive in a specific way. The evidence the AI Act will eventually demand — documentation of what each system does, logs of its outputs, a record of the human oversight applied, an account of the data it was trained and tested on — is slow to produce retroactively. You cannot log a decision that happened last year. If your programme restarts in mid-2027, the compliance file you can build will start in mid-2027, and every decision made before that is undocumented.

## The same evidence is already being demanded elsewhere

The strongest argument for continuing is not European. HR Dive reported on 23 June 2026 that Judge Rita Lin declined to dismiss FEHA claims against Workday's AI screening tools, allowing them to proceed even for non-California plaintiffs on the basis that the tools were designed, developed, maintained and controlled in California. We wrote about [what that ruling means for vendor selection](/blog/workday-ai-hiring-ruling-california/) separately.

The point for this post: litigation of that kind runs on documentation. What did the tool do to this applicant, on what inputs, reviewed by whom, against what tested alternative. That is close to the same file the AI Act's Annex III obligations describe. Building it once serves both, and one of the two is live now rather than in December 2027.

## What to do with the 16 months

Treat the delay as capacity rather than relief, and spend it on the parts that are slow.

**Finish the inventory.** Every AI system touching an employment decision, including the ones inside products you did not buy as AI: the ranking in your ATS, the matching in your internal mobility tool, the scoring in a video interview platform, the summarisation in a performance tool. Inventories take longer than anyone plans because the systems are bought by different people. Do it now, when nothing depends on it.

**Start logging.** If a system produces an output that affects a person, retain that output with its inputs and a timestamp. This is the single item you cannot backfill, and it is usually a configuration setting rather than a project.

**Fix who classifies as what.** The Act distinguishes providers from deployers, with different obligations attached. For each system in your inventory, establish which role you occupy. For anything you have configured, customised or rebadged, ask counsel whether you have drifted into provider obligations you did not intend to take on.

**Pin the vendors to the new date.** Contracts signed in 2025 will contain conformity commitments referencing 2 August 2026. Those references are now wrong. Ask each vendor in writing what they will deliver by 2 December 2027 and get it into the renewal, while your leverage is a live negotiation rather than a compliance emergency.

**Stop calling it a deadline project.** Rename it, restaff it as ongoing operations, and move it out of the programme budget that gets reviewed against milestones. A team that owns documentation and logging permanently will pass the 2027 date without noticing it. A project that reconvenes in 2027 will not.

The uncomfortable part is that nothing in the list produces a visible result this year. That is why it gets cut, and it is why doing it now is cheap.
