Workers spend around 1.5 hours a day on AI — roughly 47 days a year — and nearly half of that time goes on troubleshooting errors or refining prompts, which works out at about 20 days a year of rework. That is from a BambooHR survey of more than 1,600 full-time salaried US workers, reported by HR Dive's Ryan Golden on 2 September 2026. If your AI business case counts the 47 days and not the 20, it overstates the return by roughly the amount of the return.

## The gross number is the one that gets presented

Time-saved figures in enterprise AI cases are almost always gross. A task took 40 minutes, it now takes 10, multiply by frequency and headcount, present the annual saving. The arithmetic is fine. The measurement boundary is wrong, because it stops at the moment the output appears and excludes the time spent deciding whether the output is usable and fixing it when it is not.

BambooHR's split puts a number on the excluded part: close to half. That does not make AI unprofitable, and the survey does not say it does — 65% of respondents told BambooHR they felt confident and enthusiastic about AI. It means the honest version of your case is roughly half the size of the one currently in the deck, and the honest version is the one that survives a second year of scrutiny.

There is a practical reason to fix this before someone else does. A saving that was presented gross and then measured net looks like a failed initiative rather than a profitable one. Presenting the net figure first makes the programme defensible when finance goes looking.

## Rework is a fixable line, not a tax

Twenty days a year of troubleshooting and prompt refinement is not an inherent property of the technology. It is the cost of putting a general-purpose tool in front of a specific task and asking each person to bridge the gap themselves, repeatedly, in private.

Where that time goes is predictable. People re-prompt because the first output missed context the model was never given. They check outputs because they have no way to know which ones are reliable. They rewrite because the format was wrong. Every one of those is a design problem with an owner, and none of them is solved by more enthusiasm or another training session on prompting.

The cheapest fix is narrowing the job. A tool pointed at one task, with the context already loaded and the output shaped for what happens next, does not need to be re-prompted. We have written about [what an AI pilot actually costs](/blog/what-an-ai-pilot-costs/), including the pattern that made the biggest difference in our own hiring work: we stopped asking the model to produce a judgement and had it extract five specific fields instead. Extraction is checkable at a glance. A score is not, which is why a score generates argument and then rework.

## The knowledge-transfer finding is the sharper one

Buried in the same survey: over a third of workers told BambooHR that organisational knowledge now transfers mainly through AI tools rather than human mentorship. BambooHR also found workers spent 41% of their time interacting with AI against 59% with colleagues.

That second figure is a ratio nobody planned. The first one should worry anyone who owns succession or onboarding, because most of those plans are built on mentoring: a new joiner learns the undocumented parts of the job from someone who has done it, and a successor is developed through exposure to the person they will replace.

If a third of your workforce is getting that content from a model instead, two things follow. The knowledge being transferred is whatever was written down and indexed, which excludes the judgement that made the senior person worth learning from. And the mentoring relationships your succession plan assumes exist are not forming, while the plan continues to report green because it measures assigned mentors rather than knowledge actually moving.

This is checkable. Ask a recent joiner in a critical role who taught them the three hardest parts of the job. Ask the person named as a successor in your plan how many hours they spent with the incumbent last quarter. Neither question requires a survey and both produce an answer your talent dashboard does not have.

## What to change this quarter

**Re-present one AI business case net of rework.** Pick the initiative with the loudest savings claim, measure how long people spend checking and fixing output, and subtract it. If the case survives, you have a defensible number. If it does not, you have found out before finance did.

**Measure time to a usable output, not time to an output.** The clock stops when the work is good enough to send, not when the model finishes generating. This single change to how pilots are measured surfaces the rework automatically and kills the initiatives that were only ever fast at producing drafts.

**Own the rework as a design backlog.** Collect what people are actually re-prompting for. The pattern will be small — missing context, wrong format, unreliable in one specific case — and each item is a fix in the tool rather than a habit in the user.

**Audit whether your succession and onboarding plans still describe reality.** Not by reviewing the documents. By asking the people in them who taught them what they know.

Nothing in the BambooHR data argues for using less AI. It argues for stating the cost honestly and then removing it, which is a different and considerably more productive project than defending the gross figure for another year.
