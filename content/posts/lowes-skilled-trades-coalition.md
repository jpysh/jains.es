Lowe's Foundation launched the Building Futures Skilled Trades Coalition on 1 September with more than 75 partner organisations, targeting one million skilled tradespeople by 2035, HR Dive reported on 4 September. Named partners include Nvidia, AT&T, Bank of America, Carrier, General Motors, DeWalt and Duke Energy. It builds on a $250m commitment made in April 2026 and will invest in training and credentialing alongside shared progress benchmarks. The two words to act on are "shared benchmarks", because a group of that size agreeing common measures is how a de facto credential standard gets made, and employers outside the coalition will eventually either recognise it or not.

That recognition decision is the one you control. Whether the coalition succeeds is not.

## Why shared benchmarks are the story

Multi-employer training coalitions are common and most of them produce a press release and a scholarship fund. What separates the ones that change a labour market is whether participants agree on what a trained person has demonstrated. Once seventy-five employers, including several of the largest industrial and utility names in the US, describe competence the same way, candidates start arriving in the market holding that description. Recruiters begin using it as a filter. Training providers build to it because that is what buyers ask for.

HR Dive cited the US Department of Education's estimate that 2.1m skilled trades jobs could go unfilled by 2030, which is the shortage the coalition is aimed at. If the coalition trains even a fraction of a million people against common benchmarks, the pipeline in trades-adjacent sectors will be substantially populated by people credentialed to a standard you did not help write.

## The decision, and what each answer costs

If you employ electricians, HVAC technicians, facilities staff, industrial maintenance or construction trades, you have three options and no fourth.

**Recognise it.** You accept the credential as evidence in hiring, map it into your job architecture, and stop paying to assess or re-teach what it already establishes. The benefit is a larger qualified applicant pool at no training cost to you. The cost is that you have outsourced part of your competency definition to a group you do not sit in, and you will discover the gaps between their standard and your requirements in production.

**Recognise it partially.** The credential clears a candidate to a defined point and you assess the remainder yourself. This is usually the right answer and it is also the one that requires actual work: someone has to read the benchmarks, compare them against your own competency framework, and specify which of your internal training modules become redundant and which remain load-bearing. That is a few days of a competent L&D person's time.

**Ignore it.** Defensible if your trades requirements are genuinely specific, if your intake is small, or if you run a strong apprenticeship you have no reason to change. What is not defensible is ignoring it by default and continuing to fund internal training for competencies the market is about to certify for free.

## Why this is time-sensitive rather than a 2035 problem

The target date is 2035 and the decision is not a 2035 decision. Credential recognition becomes costly to reverse once it is embedded in job postings, hiring manager habits and your own training catalogue. If you spend the next two years building internal training for competencies the coalition is standardising, that spend is stranded and you will defend it rather than write it off.

The other time pressure is participation. Seventy-five-plus organisations is the founding group, not a closed one. If your competency requirements differ materially from what the coalition is likely to codify, arguing that inside the process is cheaper than working around the output afterwards. That is a decision for whoever owns trades talent, and it should be made this year while the benchmarks are still being written.

## What to actually do in the next quarter

Read the benchmarks when they are published and compare them against your own trades competency framework line by line. If you do not have a documented framework for these roles, that is the first piece of work, and it is required whatever you decide about the coalition.

Get a named owner for the recognition decision. In most organisations trades hiring sits with operations and site management, and L&D is not in the conversation, which is how a standard gets adopted informally by hiring managers before anyone decides whether to adopt it.

Then tell your hiring managers what the answer is. A recognition policy nobody communicates produces the worst version of this: inconsistent treatment of candidates holding the same credential, which is both an operational mess and, where it varies by who screened the application, an exposure you do not want.

Coalition credentials are not the only external money moving into this. [Federal workforce funding has become sector-targeted](/blog/where-the-workforce-training-money-went/), and construction and skilled trades are named in it, so some of the training you are budgeting for may already have a funded route.

One caution about the number. A million tradespeople by 2035 is a target, and targets of that shape are routinely missed. Base your decision on whether the benchmarks are useful to you, not on whether the headline figure is achieved. A credential adopted by seventy-five large employers changes your hiring market even if the coalition reaches a quarter of its goal.
