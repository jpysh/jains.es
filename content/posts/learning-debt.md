Twenty-nine per cent of workers said they had delivered work they could not explain if asked how they did it, according to a TalentLMS report released on 15 July and covered by HR Dive on 16 July. The survey covered 1,200 US employees aged 25 to 64. That figure is not a learning metric. It is a statement about the reviewability of output your organisation has already shipped, and it should be read by whoever owns handover, audit response and incident review, not only by whoever owns training.

TalentLMS calls the underlying condition learning debt: the gap between what a role has become and what the organisation has trained anyone to do. The report found 41% of respondents said their role evolved faster than their company's training could keep up.

## Why the 29% is the number that matters

The rest of the findings describe a gap. This one describes concealment.

Nearly 60% of respondents told TalentLMS they use AI at least sometimes to complete tasks they were never trained for. Sixty-two per cent said they used workarounds. Forty-seven per cent said they kept quiet about a skill gap, with 50% citing an expectation that they figure things out alone and 49% saying they feared looking incompetent.

Put those together and the mechanism is clear enough. An employee is given work their training does not cover. Admitting that carries a cost they have correctly assessed. AI produces something acceptable. The work ships. The gap is now invisible to every system your organisation uses to detect gaps, because the output looks fine.

A visible skills gap shows up as a missed deadline, a bad draft, a question in a team meeting. Those are all cheap signals and your management processes are built to catch them. An invisible one shows up eighteen months later when the person leaves, or when a regulator asks how a figure was derived, or when something breaks at 2am and the only person who could explain the system cannot.

## The specific failure modes

**Handover.** A leaver walks through their work with a successor. If a third of what they produced was generated and not understood, the walkthrough transfers files rather than knowledge. The successor inherits output they cannot maintain and does not know which parts those are.

**Audit and regulatory response.** Someone asks how a number was calculated, a decision was reached, or a document was produced. "I asked an AI and it looked right" is not an answer that survives contact with an auditor, and the person giving it will not have been the one who decided to accept that risk.

**Incident review.** A post-mortem depends on people being able to reconstruct what they did and why. Reconstruction fails where the reasoning was never held by a human in the first place.

**Capability planning.** Your skills matrix says the competency is covered because the work got done. It is not covered. Every workforce plan built on that matrix is wrong in a direction you cannot see.

## What to add in the next quarter

One change does most of the work: make explicability a review criterion on anything that matters.

Concretely, for work that feeds a regulated output, a customer commitment, a financial figure or a system other people depend on, the reviewer asks the owner to walk through how it was produced and why the approach is right. Not whether AI was involved. That question invites a defensive answer and tells you nothing. The question is whether the person who owns the work can account for it.

Three things make this work rather than becoming theatre.

**It has to be routine and universal, not triggered by suspicion.** If the walkthrough only happens when a manager is worried, it functions as an accusation and people will prepare for it rather than answer honestly.

**Not being able to answer has to be survivable.** The TalentLMS numbers say half of people stay quiet because they expect to be judged. A check that punishes the honest answer will produce dishonest ones and you will have spent effort making the problem harder to see.

**It has to produce a training input.** When someone cannot explain their work, that is the most precise needs-analysis signal your L&D function will ever receive: a named person, a named task, a specific gap, discovered at the moment it mattered. If that finding does not route anywhere, the check is pure overhead and should be dropped.

## What this does not justify

It does not justify restricting AI use, monitoring tools, or requiring disclosure of every AI-assisted task. The behaviour TalentLMS documents is a rational response to being handed work nobody trained you for, and the employees are not the problem in the chain. Sixty per cent of people using AI for untrained tasks is what a capable workforce does when the alternative is failing publicly.

It also does not justify a new training programme as the first move. You cannot write curriculum for a gap you have not located, and the located gaps are sitting inside work that already shipped. Find them first.

There is a second problem sitting next to this one. Where companies are training on AI at all, the training tends to stop at basic literacy and prompting, which is a level below where the performance difference actually appears. That is covered in [our piece on why AI training stops at literacy](/blog/ai-training-stops-at-literacy/), and the two compound: people are using AI for work they were not trained for, with AI skills that were not trained to the level the work requires.
