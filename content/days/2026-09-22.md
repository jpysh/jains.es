## The intersection

**Your language is not missing. It is unmerged.**

Yesterday's number was that Indian languages cost an average of 8.0 times
what English costs under `cl100k_base`, measured across fourteen languages
([arXiv 2607.24276, 27 July 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24276)). Today,
the mechanism, because it changes what you can ask for.

A tokeniser's vocabulary begins with all 256 possible bytes. Every byte your
keyboard produces is in there on the first line. Nothing about Devanagari or
Tamil is absent.

What the vocabulary then does is glue pairs together. Look at a large pile of
text, find the two adjacent pieces that occur together most often, glue them,
repeat about a hundred thousand times. That is byte-pair encoding, and that is
all of it. A merge is a count, not a judgement.

So your text comes out in many small pieces not because your script is
missing, but because the merges that would have joined its bytes never
happened. Nobody rejected your language. Nobody looked at it.

That distinction is the difference between a problem with your script and a
problem with somebody's corpus. Only one of those is fixable, and it is the
second one.

The evidence that it is fixable is already measured. Swap in a tokeniser built
on a wider corpus and the average Indic penalty falls by 73%, on the same
sentences ([arXiv 2607.24276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24276)). The bytes did
not change. The merges did.

## What I learned today

Day 2 of building language models from scratch, in public.

- The vocabulary is a file you can open. `enc._mergeable_ranks` gives you
  every byte sequence and the rank at which it was merged. The first fifty
  entries tell you what the training text was.
  ([What a merge is](/wiki/what-a-merge-is/))
- Rank is the interesting column, not the token. Rank 0 merged first, which
  means it was the most common pair in the corpus. Reading the ranks is
  reading the corpus.
  ([Tokenisation and the cost of your language](/wiki/tokenisation-and-the-cost-of-your-language/))
- **What I am least sure about:** a Devanagari character is three bytes in
  UTF-8. When a token holds two of those three, I do not know whether to count
  that as one failed merge or two. My number changes with the answer, and the
  number is the thing I am putting on screen.
- Yesterday's open question is closed. I had asked whether the honest
  multiplier is tokens per sentence or tokens per character. The paper defines
  fertility, so the comparison has a convention and I was not free to pick.

## Useful

**Read the vocabulary yourself.** Yesterday's tool counted tokens. Today's
does something stranger: it opens the table that decides the count.

```
pip install tiktoken
python -c "import tiktoken; \
r = tiktoken.get_encoding('cl100k_base')._mergeable_ranks; \
print([p for p,_ in sorted(r.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1])][:50])"
```

That prints the first fifty merges ever made, in order. It is the closest
thing to a photograph of the text this tokeniser was built from, and it takes
about thirty seconds. Then look for the first entry in your own script, and
note its rank.
([tiktoken, on GitHub](https://github.com/openai/tiktoken))

## Number of the day

**89%.** The reduction in tokenisation inequality that a modified byte-pair
encoding achieves against classical BPE, reported in a preprint by Negar
Foroutan and six co-authors. Their change is one line of the algorithm:
instead of merging the most
common pair overall, merge the pair that most helps whichever language is
currently worst compressed. They report negligible impact on overall
compression.
([arXiv 2508.04796, 6 August 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04796))
